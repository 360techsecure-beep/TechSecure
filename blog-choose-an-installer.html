How to Choose a Security Camera Installer in Los Angeles

The best security camera quote is not necessarily the one with the most cameras or the lowest total. It is the one that clearly connects each camera to a coverage goal, identifies the equipment and work included, explains how recordings will be stored and accessed, and defines what you receive when the installation is complete.

Use the same written checklist with every installer. That turns three very different proposals into something you can compare line by line.

1. Start with the problem the system must solve

Before discussing brands or megapixels, write down the incidents and areas that matter: a front approach, package handoff, parking entrance, register, side gate, warehouse aisle, or shared hallway. Ask the installer to explain what each proposed camera should let you see and at what distance.

A wide overview and an identification view do different jobs. A quote should distinguish them instead of promising that one camera will capture everything. For larger properties, request a marked plan showing camera locations, view direction, recorder location, and the main cable routes.

2. Compare the equipment by model and purpose

Every proposal should identify the camera and recorder model numbers, lens or field of view, recording resolution, night-vision approach, weather rating where relevant, storage capacity, and whether licenses or subscriptions are required. “Eight 4K cameras” is not enough detail to compare two systems.

Ask whether the proposed cameras and recorder appear in the manufacturer's current support information and who provides firmware updates. If interoperability matters, verify claimed ONVIF conformance in ONVIF's official product database rather than relying only on a logo in a proposal.

3. Make the installation scope visible

A complete quote should state how cables will be routed and protected, who supplies switches or power supplies, whether patching or painting is included, how exterior penetrations will be sealed, what cleanup is included, and how changes are approved. For Wi-Fi equipment, the scope should still identify power, signal testing, recording destination, and what happens during an internet outage.

For residential work, California's Contractors State License Board says a home-improvement contract should describe the work, materials, equipment, payment schedule, and responsibility for any permits. Its official contract guidance also recommends putting promised changes in writing. Requirements depend on the project, so confirm which rules and license classifications apply to your job.

4. Verify the business information independently

Do not treat a license number printed on a proposal as verification. Use the CSLB license-check service to review the current record when a California contractor license is required for the work. CSLB also recommends comparing written bids on the same plans, specifications, and scope instead of automatically choosing the lowest bid.

Ask how property damage and worker injuries are handled, and verify any insurance information relevant to the project. For references or portfolio examples, look for work similar to your building type and ask what was actually included. Photos alone do not show whether playback, storage, remote access, and handoff were completed correctly.

5. Compare recording and storage, not just live video

Ask each installer to state the recording mode for every camera, the estimated retention target, what assumptions produced that estimate, and how the final result will be checked. Camera count, average bitrate, scene activity, frame rate, compression, and continuous versus event recording all affect retention.

Our footage-retention planning guide explains how to work backward from the incident-discovery window. If the system uses a local DVR or NVR, the quote should also describe drive capacity, overwrite behavior, storage-health alerts, and how to export an important clip.

6. Require a secure owner handoff

The owner should control the administrator account, recovery email address, and any cloud subscription. The installer should replace default credentials, create separate user accounts where appropriate, and explain who can view live video, export recordings, or change settings. The FTC's security-camera guidance recommends unique passwords, software updates, and two-factor authentication when available.

Ask whether remote access uses the manufacturer's supported service, a VPN, or another documented method. Avoid proposals that depend on unexplained open internet ports or an installer's permanent shared administrator login.

7. Put the acceptance test in the quote

Before final payment, test the system at the recorder and on the owner's authorized devices. A useful acceptance checklist includes:

  • Each camera name matches the final site map.
  • Day and night views cover the agreed purpose.
  • The recorder clock, time zone, and recording schedule are correct.
  • Playback works for every camera and an export opens on another device.
  • The oldest available footage is consistent with the planned retention.
  • Remote access works without giving the installer sole control of the account.
  • Cables, switch ports, and power supplies are labeled.
  • The owner receives the equipment list, configuration backup, and support contact.

Questions to ask every installer

  • What does each camera need to identify or document?
  • Which exact models, lenses, recorder, storage drives, switches, and subscriptions are included?
  • How will cables and exterior penetrations be routed, protected, and finished?
  • How many days of footage is the system designed to retain, and what assumptions support that estimate?
  • Who owns every administrator and cloud account after handoff?
  • What testing, training, documentation, labor coverage, and manufacturer warranties are included in writing?
  • How are scope changes priced and approved?

Red flags that make a quote hard to trust

  • No property walkthrough or plan for coverage.
  • Generic equipment descriptions without model numbers.
  • No explanation of recording, retention, or clip export.
  • Pressure to decide immediately or promises made only verbally.
  • Requests to share a permanent administrator password.
  • No written process for changes, testing, owner training, or follow-up service.
